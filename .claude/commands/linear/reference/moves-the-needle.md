# Moves the needle

> **What this is**: the one judgement stride uses to decide whether something learned from the work is worth acting on. [`/linear:plan-work`](../plan-work.md) asks it before filing a card, [carry lessons](carry-lessons.md) asks it before changing the cards still in Todo, and [`/vision-evolve`](../../../skills/vision-evolve/SKILL.md) asks it before adding a line to the Vision.

Acting on a learning always costs the person running stride some attention: a card to read and judge, a card in Todo that changed under them, a Vision line to keep true. The judgement is whether the learning earns that attention.

## A learning that recurs and points to a better way of working earns action

<mark>**One occurrence is a fact. A repeat is a coincidence. A pattern is something you can act on.**</mark> The same problem seen again and again shows what the fix should be, and that the problem is real. A learning that keeps coming back and clearly points to a better way of working survives. Act on it.

This is the idea behind "recurrence reveals reality": each repeat tells you more about the shape of the problem, so a count of one tells you very little. The number isn't a rule. Two repeats can be enough when the fix is plain, and four can fall short when nobody can say what to change.

## A one-off is named in one line and left

Something seen once is named in one line, with its reason, and nothing else happens. The line keeps the finding visible, so it can be picked up if it comes back, and it costs the reader a moment instead of a card.

## A one-off that cost a lot still earns action

Cost beats count. Act on a learning seen once when it was expensive even once:

- **A safety risk**, such as a step that could delete or overwrite work without asking
- **Work silently lost**, such as an output that was dropped and nothing said so
- **Something wrong put in front of a reader**, such as a card or document that states what the code doesn't do

These are signs for judgement, not a checklist. A problem seen once can still make the case, and one that fits a sign can still fail it.

## Evidence comes before the case

The case is made from three answers, and they are written down where a reader can judge them:

- **How often has it happened?** Name the occurrences: the recordings, cards or runs.
- **What does it cost?** Say what it cost the person: time, a wrong result, lost work.
- **What happens if nothing is done?** Say whether it gets worse, stays as it is, or fixes itself.

A case with no occurrences named and no cost named is a thin case, however strong the idea feels.

## Worked examples

The first two come from 8 November 2026, when 19 follow-up cards were filed from one batch of 33 recordings and the same agent later recommended cancelling 7 of them. The last two are made-up illustrations of the other two cases.

- **Worth acting on:** slide matching failed on recording after recording. The card names the recordings and the cost of each failure.
- **Named in one line and left:** a meeting layout changed on one recording of 33, and the job coped. The output says: "Left out: second crop for layout changes, one recording, job coped."
- **Acted on though seen once (illustration):** a job drops a recording's audio and says nothing. It happened once, but work was lost silently, so it earns a card.
- **Left though it recurred (illustration):** the same cosmetic wording slip in three logs, with nothing a reader loses. Three occurrences and no cost add up to a thin case.
